If you are like me, you have lately been reexamining your relationship with your phone, and with that social media most likely. At this point
the two are intrinsically linked together. Social media apps have been pushing hard to make their services only usable on the
mobile apps. Instagram's web app is pretty horrid and slow, Reddit seems to have made old.reddit.com just show the same 25 posts
reordered on refresh, and the current Reddit design is a masterclass in terribleness. On the apps though they tend to work just fine. So these apps create a codependency 
on your phone, and you can literally scroll miles and miles before stopping. So what happens if you try to leave these apps after years of heavy usage?

This all started about a year ago. I had Instagram, Reddit, and Bluesky on my phone as dedicated apps. Bluesky was much less of a problem because they, 
thankfully, have not enshittified things (yet). I could lose hours to Reddit and Instagram (lets just call them _the apps_ now). In between sets at
the gym, I open up one of the apps. Waiting in line, open an app. Hell I was so bad I would open an app at a red light. Fucking crazy. Finally, an ad on Instagram of all places,
got me interesting in one of those RFID app blockers. I had tried blocking apps before but they were easy to get around so I figured this would be better. And it was! I installed their
app, got the card set up, and locked down the apps. They had 3 little 5 minute breaks you could take to use the apps, or you could break your streak for longer. I went about 5 months of 
no apps and uninstalled both apps until a [really rough winter storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_23–27,_2026_North_American_winter_storm) hit Nashville. Megathreads popped up and one person, who himself was without power,
started running some scripts to interpret the data Nashville Electric was providing as a way to find trends for power restoration. Ok, I thought, let me unblock and reinstall reddit so 
I can read these threads while I didn't have power. That's how Reddit slithered back into my life. Instagram though didn't come back in the same way. So on the one hand I am free! But am I?

_And yes, I know I can easily go back and reblock Reddit. Unfortunately, I moderate 2 communities on Reddit, and one requires active moderation for now._

But what happens when you drop off a social media platform like Instagram? I used to be an active poster. I have created a niche for myself by being someone
who photographs Nashville's bike scene. I would go to a race, edit photos, and then blast them on Instagram. I would get all the likes, comments, a few bucks, and do it again the next week.
In between I would post stupid shit to my story, repost things I thought were important. But when I blocked the app, I just went silent. I didnt make an announcement but instead dropped off the face 
of the earth. And the hardest part looking back is that no one really noticed. It hit hardest a few weeks ago when the racing season started and I didnt go. I havent felt an urge to really go to the races
this year due to burn out, stress, and general life getting in the way. For someone who photographed the races consistently for 4 years you'd think that someone would reach out but no one has. This isn't a pity party. 
This leads up to my thinking here about the third death. 

So while I was out photographing another bike event last week, it hit me that there is a new third death emerging. I say third because there is already two "defined" deaths that occur for someone:

1. The physical death you will eventually experience
2. The metaphysical death when someone says your name for the last time

Number 2 hits harder because for 99.99% of us we will fade into obscurity. However, I think, if the concept of the third death catches on, that may be the most brutal of them all. The idea of the social death.

You begin a social life when you add people on Instagram and interact with them. This type of friendship is most likely a shallow friendship. You will like their posts, maybe leave a comment, share a funny reel, and then go about your day. On
some level this is totally ok! Not every friendship needs to be the kind where you get up at 3am to find them on the side of the road with a flat tire. The flip side though is that these friendships are surface level at best, and the moment you
you aren't on someone else's front page, you can be easily forgotten in the shuffle of life. Which brings us to the term social death: **the death you die when you drop off social media and the shallow friendships dry up.** What makes
it more complicated is that unlike a physical death, or the metaphysical death of your name being said for the last time, the only thing lost is connection, but connections are cheap when it comes to social media, so what did we 
really lose? We lost our social identity, and a bit of dopamine when we'd see a post take off and people enjoying the content. We lost those simple connections because removing yourself from
the platform removes an ingrained way of communication (Instagram DMs, Facebook Messenger, WhatsApp, etc). The connections social media allows us to make aren't strong enough on their own to withstand an event like
someone removing themselves from the apps.

Last week I went out and shot a bike event. No Instagram waiting for the photos.
It was more fun than it's been in a while. But it was also weird. I genuinely didn't know who would see them, or if anyone would. 
After years of the app telling me exactly how many people cared, not knowing felt almost disorienting. Maybe that's what life after a 
social death actually looks like. Not grief. Just quiet, and having to figure out if the thing you were doing was ever really for you.
